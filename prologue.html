CARRIERS

For 150 years, humans have taught objects to remember sound. It is the same trick every time: a surface moving past a reader at constant speed. Only the marks change. Below, one two-and-a-half-second sound is held five different ways.

Four notes travel down this page — carved, wiggled, magnetized, counted, and finally set loose.
The signal · A minor seventh, ascending · playhead sweeps on play
01 · 1877 · WAX CYLINDER · sound as depth

Carve it.

Edison's phonograph cut sound vertically — "hill and dale." Loud moments gouged deeper valleys into the wax; quiet ones barely scratched the surface. A needle dropped into yesterday's groove pushed the air the same way a voice once did.

No electricity in the playback. No code. Just geometry, remembered.

02 · 1948 · MICROGROOVE LP · sound as wiggle

Turn the cut sideways.

The long-playing record moved the signal into the horizontal: the groove swings left and right while its depth stays steady, and the stylus is thrown side to side thousands of times a second. Later, stereo split the difference — one channel per groove wall, at 45 degrees each.

Unrolled, the groove on a single LP side runs roughly half a kilometer. You own sound by the meter.

Groove from above · lateral displacement = signal · walls in silver
03 · 1935→1963 · MAGNETIC TAPE · sound as rust

Persuade the rust.

Tape is ferric oxide — rust — glued to plastic. As it streams past the record head, billions of microscopic particles are nudged into alignment with the signal's magnetic field, and they simply stay that way. Nothing is carved. The mark is invisible.

It was the first carrier you could erase — which made it the first you could re-record, overdub, splice. The recording studio as an instrument begins here.

Oxide particle orientation · field strength = signal · head at right
04 · 1982 · COMPACT DISC · sound as arithmetic

Count it.

The disc stops imitating the wave and starts measuring it: 44,100 readings per second, each written as a 16-bit number, stamped as pits in polycarbonate and read by a laser. The trick most people never hear about — a pit isn't a one. The edges are the ones. Flat stretches, pit or land, are runs of zeros.

The rainbow on a disc's surface isn't ink. It's the data itself, spaced so finely it diffracts light.

Pit/land track · shown at 4-bit so you can watch · transitions = 1 · λ 780nm
05 · NOW · THE FILE · sound with no body

Let go of the object.

The final step removes the surface entirely. The numbers no longer need a dedicated body — they live wherever numbers live, copied perfectly, endlessly, nowhere in particular. Sound becomes weightless.

But everything above this line is dying. Wax softens, vinyl wears, oxide sheds from its backing, discs rot from the edges in. No carrier lasts; sound survives only by moving to the next one before the last one fails. An archive isn't a vault. It's a relay.
