Shellac Is Bugs

The first mass medium of recorded sound — every 78, every Caruso, every early Armstrong side — was pressed from the secretions of a scale insect. The dawn of the record industry was, materially speaking, a harvest.

Essays on the matter that holds sound

Somewhere in a climate-controlled vault, on an archival shelf, in an acid-free sleeve, rests a disc containing Enrico Caruso's voice — one of the most valuable recorded artifacts on Earth. It is made of insect resin. Not figuratively, not partially by some trace additive: the material that binds that disc together, the substance the industry was named for when people said shellac the way they later said vinyl, is the processed secretion of Kerria lacca, a scale insect the size of an apple seed that lives on trees in India and Southeast Asia. For the first half-century of the record business, the entire commercial catalog of human music rode on the metabolism of a bug.

The harvest

The insect does not know it is in the music business. Female lac insects colonize the branches of host trees by the tens of thousands, sink their mouthparts in, drink sap, and excrete a hard amber resin that fuses into a communal crust — a tunnel-city of lacquer encasing the whole colony. Farmers cut these encrusted twigs, called sticklac, then crush, sieve, and wash them, melt and filter the resin, and stretch it into thin sheets that shatter into the translucent flakes of commerce. It takes a staggering census of insects to yield a pound of the stuff — and the language kept the receipt. The word lac shares its root with lakh, the South Asian unit meaning one hundred thousand. The material is named for the size of the crowd that made it. Before the record industry ever pressed a disc, it had inherited a supply chain of trees, seasons, monsoons, and multitudes.

Humans had used shellac for centuries — as furniture finish, as sealing wax on the envelope, as the gleam on a jelly bean, which it still is: food-grade shellac is the "confectioner's glaze" on candy and the coating on pharmaceutical pills. When Emile Berliner's flat gramophone disc needed a pressable material in the 1890s — hard rubber having proven unreliable — the answer came from, of all places, a button factory, where shellac compounds were already being molded into durable little goods. The disc, at birth, was a very large button with a spiral on it.

The material is named for the size of the crowd that made it: lac, from lakh — one hundred thousand.

Mostly rock, held together by bugs

Here is the second surprise, after the entomology: a 78 is not mostly shellac. The resin is the binder, typically a modest fraction of the compound; the bulk of the disc is mineral filler — finely ground rock and limestone — plus carbon black for color and cotton flock for strength. A 78 is closer to a thin, precisely molded slab of engineered stone than to a sheet of plastic, which is why it weighs what it weighs and shatters how it shatters.

The filler was not merely cheap bulk. In the steel-needle era, the disc and the needle were designed as a consumable pair: the compound was deliberately abrasive, grinding each fresh needle to the exact profile of the groove within the first revolutions, whereupon the needle was to be discarded after a side or two. The record sharpened its own reader. Some of the surface noise we now hear as the romantic crackle of the era is simply the sound of the stone in the disc — the medium audibly declaring its ingredients on every play. Fidelity and material were never separate questions; the hiss is the limestone.

Breakage

Everyone who has handled a 78 knows the material's temper: drop one and it doesn't bounce, doesn't crack — it explodes into shards like a dinner plate. "Breakage" was a standing line item in the economics of distribution, records dying by the crate in transit. And the supply chain behind the material was as fragile as the disc: when the Second World War severed shipping from India, the American industry faced a genuine shellac famine. The response was one of the stranger recycling programs in industrial history — scrap drives where the public donated old records to be ground up and re-pressed into new ones. Wartime pressings literally contain particles of earlier, unknown music, pulverized and redistributed. Somewhere in the noise floor of a 1943 disc are the ghosts of whatever got melted to make it.

The shortage did one more thing: it forced the industry to experiment seriously with a petroleum-based alternative it had been circling for years — and unbreakable discs shipped to troops overseas made the case vividly. The bug had a successor. That story is Nº 04.

The outliving

Now the twist this series keeps arriving at from different directions. Shellac, the brittle, noisy, insect-based, stone-filled, nineteenth-century compound — the material every subsequent format was an improvement upon — has turned out to be one of the most chemically durable carriers ever mass-produced. Kept flat and unbroken, a century-old 78 is typically stable: the resin does not hydrolyze like polyurethane binder, does not sour like acetate film, does not fade like organic dye. Its enemies are gravity, careless hands, and the wear of a thousand steel needles — mechanical deaths, visible ones, honest ones. Chemically, the bug resin simply abides.

There is a version of media history that reads as a steady march of progress, each format superior to the last. The shelf tells a different story. On the shelf, the 1910 disc made of insects and limestone waits patiently, playable, while formats sixty and eighty years its junior — the premium tape of Nº 02, the burned disc of Nº 05 — quietly decompose beside it. Progress optimized for capacity, fidelity, convenience; almost never, until recently, for time. The lac insect, who was optimizing for nothing but the protection of her colony, accidentally out-engineered most of the twentieth century.

So the next time a needle drops into a coarse groove and a voice from 1923 comes up through the crackle, consider the full chain of custody: a colony on a branch, a monsoon season, a harvest, a button factory's recipe, a pressing plant, a hundred years of shelves. Sound as agriculture. The first archive was grown.

A NECESSARY ASTERISK · Not every coarse-groove disc is shellac — the era's edges are full of exceptions (laminated pressings, early plastics, Edison's very different Diamond Discs), and instantaneous lacquer discs are another animal entirely, with a menace all their own (Nº 06). And the abrasive-compound-plus-steel-needle bargain means historical discs were designed to be worn; modern transfer practice, with sized styli and gentle tracking, is an act of diplomacy the medium never expected.
